# Executive Adoption Model

## Start bounded; scale with evidence

Infrastructure as a Product should be introduced as a measurable product and operating-model change, not as a mandate to replace the enterprise technology estate.

## Phase 1 — Select the proving ground

Choose one infrastructure product or customer journey with:

- visible demand;
- repeated delivery friction or inconsistency;
- executive relevance;
- manageable blast radius;
- a known consumer population;
- existing authority to operate in the target environment; and
- baseline data that allows comparison with today's process.

Avoid selecting an artificially easy demo that cannot answer whether the model works under meaningful governance and operational constraints.

## Phase 2 — Establish ownership and authority

Before implementation, name:

- executive sponsor;
- product owner;
- responsible engineering owner;
- security/control authority;
- operational support owner;
- approving authority for material actions; and
- consumer representative.

No automation should be permitted to fill an ownership gap.

## Phase 3 — Define the product and measures

Define the requested outcome, stable contract, supported profiles, lifecycle behavior, support boundaries, evidence requirements, and target measures.

Establish baseline metrics before claiming improvement.

## Phase 4 — Validate in stages

Progress from assessment and simulation through read-only discovery, sandbox, pilot, and production consideration according to the risk and authority available.

Leadership should require explicit gates rather than treating technical success as automatic permission to advance.

## Phase 5 — Review value and risk

At the end of the pilot, review:

- delivery lead time versus baseline;
- policy and exception results;
- reliability and recovery;
- evidence completeness;
- support demand;
- cost-to-serve;
- consumer adoption and feedback;
- security findings;
- unresolved dependencies; and
- ongoing staffing and operating cost.

## Phase 6 — Scale, change, or stop

Possible outcomes are deliberately broader than "success":

- **Scale** — value is material and risks are controlled.
- **Refine** — the model is useful but requires product, workflow, integration, or operating-model changes.
- **Constrain** — use IaaP only for product categories where the economics or risk profile justify it.
- **Stop** — evidence does not justify continued investment.

## Executive governance cadence

A lightweight steering cadence should review outcomes rather than implementation minutiae. Leadership attention is most useful on:

- product adoption and value;
- material risk and exceptions;
- authority or organizational blockers;
- investment and cost-to-serve;
- product roadmap and retirement decisions; and
- whether the next increment still supports the original business case.

## Principle

> **The goal is not to prove the architecture can work. The goal is to determine whether the organization should operate infrastructure this way.**
